**Problem.** One hundred fair dice are rolled. What is the probability that their sum is divisible
by $7$?

**Answer.**

Let $\{X_n\}$ be i.i.d. uniform on $\{1, \dots, 6\}$ and write $S_n = X_1 + \cdots + X_n$.

### Method 1: Symmetry and a one-step recursion

Let $a_n = \mathbb{P}(S_n \equiv 0 \bmod 7)$.

First, the six nonzero residues are interchangeable. Multiplying by any unit $c \in \mathbb{Z}_7^\times$
permutes $\{1, \dots, 6\}$ and therefore leaves the law of each $X_i$ unchanged, hence leaves the law
of $S_n$ unchanged. Since $\mathbb{Z}_7^\times$ acts transitively on the nonzero residues,
$\mathbb{P}(S_n \equiv r)$ takes a common value for every $r \neq 0$, namely

$$
\mathbb{P}(S_n \equiv r) = \frac{1 - a_n}{6}, \qquad r \not\equiv 0 .
$$

Now condition on the first $n$ dice. If they total $s$, the next die moves the sum to
$s+1, \dots, s+6$, which are six **distinct** residues modulo $7$ and therefore miss exactly one
value, namely $s$ itself. So the new total is divisible by $7$ with probability $1/6$ when
$s \not\equiv 0$, and with probability $0$ when $s \equiv 0$. Hence

$$
a_{n+1} = \frac{1 - a_n}{6}.
$$

The map has fixed point $1/7$, and subtracting it linearises the recursion,

$$
a_{n+1} - \frac{1}{7} = \frac{1 - a_n}{6} - \frac{1}{7} = -\frac{1}{6}\left(a_n - \frac{1}{7}\right).
$$

Since $a_1 = 0$, iterating gives

$$
a_n = \frac{1}{7}\left(1 + (-1)^n 6^{\,1-n}\right),
$$

and at $n = 100$ this is $\frac{1}{7}\left(1 + 6^{-99}\right)$.

### Method 2: Roots of unity filter

Let $\omega = e^{2\pi i/7}$. The standard indicator is

$$
\frac{1}{7}\sum_{j=0}^{6} \omega^{jm} =
\begin{cases}
1 & 7 \mid m, \\
0 & \text{otherwise,}
\end{cases}
$$

so that, using independence,

$$
a_n = \frac{1}{7}\sum_{j=0}^{6} \mathbb{E}\left[\omega^{j S_n}\right]
    = \frac{1}{7}\sum_{j=0}^{6} \left(\mathbb{E}\left[\omega^{j X}\right]\right)^n .
$$

The term $j = 0$ contributes $1$. For $j \neq 0$ the exponents $\omega^{j}, \dots, \omega^{6j}$ run
over all seven roots of unity except $1$, and those seven sum to zero, so

$$
\mathbb{E}\left[\omega^{j X}\right] = \frac{1}{6}\sum_{k=1}^{6} \omega^{jk} = \frac{0 - 1}{6} = -\frac{1}{6}.
$$

All six nonzero characters give the same value, and therefore

$$
a_n = \frac{1}{7}\left(1 + 6\left(-\frac{1}{6}\right)^{n}\right) = \frac{1}{7}\left(1 + (-1)^n 6^{\,1-n}\right).
$$

The character sum vanishing and the faces covering the nonzero residues are the same fact wearing
different clothes.

### Method 3: Spectrum of the transition matrix

The residue $S_n \bmod 7$ is a random walk on $\mathbb{Z}_7$ whose step is uniform on the nonzero
elements. Its transition matrix is

$$
P = \frac{J - I}{6},
$$

where $J$ is the all-ones matrix. Now $J$ has eigenvalue $7$ on the constant vector and $0$ on its
orthogonal complement, so $P$ has eigenvalue $\frac{7-1}{6} = 1$ on constants and
$\frac{0-1}{6} = -\frac{1}{6}$ on the six-dimensional complement.

Split the initial distribution $\delta_0$ into its uniform part and the rest, $\delta_0 = u + (\delta_0 - u)$
with $u = (1/7, \dots, 1/7)$. The first piece is fixed by $P$ and the second is scaled by $-1/6$ at
every step, so

$$
\delta_0 P^n = u + \left(-\frac{1}{6}\right)^n (\delta_0 - u).
$$

Reading off the coordinate at $0$,

$$
a_n = \frac{1}{7} + \left(-\frac{1}{6}\right)^n \left(1 - \frac{1}{7}\right)
    = \frac{1}{7}\left(1 + 6\left(-\frac{1}{6}\right)^{n}\right).
$$

The spectral gap $|-1/6| = 1/6$ is what makes the convergence to uniform so brutally fast.

### Method 4: An idempotent in the group algebra

Work in the group algebra of $\mathbb{Z}_7$, where convolution of distributions is multiplication.
Let $\delta_r$ be the point mass at $r$ and $u = \frac{1}{7}\sum_{r} \delta_r$ the uniform law. The
law of one die is

$$
g = \frac{1}{6}\sum_{r \neq 0} \delta_r = \frac{1}{6}\left(7u - \delta_0\right).
$$

The point of $u$ is that it is idempotent, $u^2 = u$, and absorbing, $u \, \delta_r = u$. So in the
binomial expansion of $(7u - \delta_0)^n$ every term containing at least one factor $u$ collapses to
a multiple of $u$, and

$$
\begin{aligned}
(7u - \delta_0)^n
&= (-1)^n \delta_0 + \left(\sum_{k=1}^{n} \binom{n}{k} 7^k (-1)^{n-k}\right) u \\
&= (-1)^n \delta_0 + \left((7-1)^n - (-1)^n\right) u \\
&= (-1)^n \delta_0 + \left(6^n - (-1)^n\right) u .
\end{aligned}
$$

The coefficient of $\delta_0$ in $g^n = 6^{-n}(7u - \delta_0)^n$ picks up $1$ from $\delta_0$ and
$1/7$ from $u$, giving

$$
a_n = \frac{1}{6^n}\left((-1)^n + \frac{6^n - (-1)^n}{7}\right) = \frac{1}{7}\left(1 + (-1)^n 6^{\,1-n}\right)
$$

once more.

### Sanity checks

All four routes agree on

$$
a_n = \frac{1}{7}\left(1 + (-1)^n 6^{\,1-n}\right),
$$

which is easy to test by hand at the bottom.

| $n$ | formula        | direct count                                     |
| --- | -------------- | ------------------------------------------------ |
| $1$ | $0$            | a single die is never $\equiv 0$                 |
| $2$ | $\frac{1}{6}$  | only $S = 7$ works, and $\mathbb{P}(S=7) = 6/36$ |
| $3$ | $\frac{5}{36}$ | $S \in \{7, 14\}$, each with $15/216$            |

### How small is the correction

At $n = 100$ the deviation from $1/7$ is $\frac{1}{7} 6^{-99}$, and $6^{99} \approx 1.09 \times 10^{77}$, so

$$
a_{100} - \frac{1}{7} \approx 1.3 \times 10^{-78}.
$$

The answer is $1/7$ correct to seventy-seven decimal places. No simulation will ever see the
difference, which is the real lesson of the problem. Note also that the sign alternates: a die can
never contribute $0$ modulo $7$, so an odd number of dice is very slightly repelled from residue $0$
and an even number very slightly attracted to it.
