# Problem.

A gambler starts with $i$ dollars and on each game wins a dollar with probability $p$ or loses one with probability $q = 1-p$, where $0 < p < 1$. He stops on reaching $N$ dollars or on losing everything. What is the probability that he ends up with $N$?

# Solution.

Writing $P_i$ for that probability,

$$
P_i = \begin{cases}\dfrac{1-(q/p)^{\,i}}{1-(q/p)^{\,N}}, & p \neq q, \\[2ex] \dfrac{i}{N}, & p = q = \tfrac12 .\end{cases}
$$

### Method 1: The difference equation

Condition on the first game,

$$
P_i = p\,P_{i+1} + q\,P_{i-1}, \qquad 1 \le i \le N-1, \qquad P_0 = 0, \quad P_N = 1 .
$$

The step that makes this easy is to write the left side as $(p+q)P_i$ and rearrange, which puts consecutive differences in a fixed ratio:

$$
p\left(P_{i+1}-P_i\right) = q\left(P_i-P_{i-1}\right), \qquad\text{that is}\qquad P_{i+1}-P_i = \frac{q}{p}\left(P_i-P_{i-1}\right) .
$$

So the differences are geometric. Since $P_0 = 0$ the first of them is $P_1$, giving $P_i - P_{i-1} = (q/p)^{\,i-1}P_1$, and summing telescopes to

$$
P_i = P_1 \sum_{j=0}^{i-1}\left(\frac{q}{p}\right)^{j} .
$$

The condition $P_N = 1$ now fixes $P_1$. For $p \neq q$ the two geometric sums divide to give the stated formula, and for $p = q$ every term of the sum is $1$, so $P_i = i P_1$ with $P_1 = 1/N$.

### Method 2: A martingale

Let $\tau$ be the first time the fortune $S_n$ reaches $0$ or $N$. Optional stopping needs to know that this happens at all, which is worth isolating.

**Lemma.** $\tau < \infty$ almost surely, and in fact $\mathbb E[\tau] \le N/p^{\,N} < \infty$.

**Proof.** Cut time into blocks of $N$ games. If the game is still running when a block begins, the fortune lies between $1$ and $N-1$, so $N$ consecutive wins would carry it above $N$; since it moves one dollar at a time it must land on $N$ first and stop. Whatever has happened before, a block consists of $N$ wins with probability $p^{\,N} > 0$, so

$$
\mathbb P\left(\tau > (k+1)N\right) \le \left(1-p^{\,N}\right)\mathbb P\left(\tau > kN\right), \qquad\text{hence}\qquad \mathbb P\left(\tau > kN\right) \le \left(1-p^{\,N}\right)^{k} .
$$

As $0 < p < 1$ the right side tends to $0$, so $\tau$ is finite almost surely. The tail decays geometrically, and summing it gives $\mathbb E[\tau] = \sum_{n\ge0}\mathbb P(\tau > n) \le N\sum_{k\ge0}(1-p^{\,N})^{k} = N/p^{\,N}$. $\blacksquare$

The bound is crude, but finiteness is all that is required.

When $p = q$ the fortune is itself a martingale. The stopped process $S_{n \wedge \tau}$ takes values in $[0,N]$ and $\tau$ is finite by the lemma, so dominated convergence carries $i = \mathbb E\left[S_{n\wedge\tau}\right]$ to the limit and

$$
i = \mathbb E\left[S_\tau\right] = N P_i + 0 \cdot (1-P_i) \implies P_i = \frac{i}{N} .
$$

When $p \neq q$ the fortune is no longer a martingale, but $(q/p)^{S_n}$ is:

$$
\mathbb E\left[\left(\tfrac{q}{p}\right)^{S_{n+1}} \,\middle|\, S_n\right] = \left(\tfrac{q}{p}\right)^{S_n}\left(p \cdot \tfrac{q}{p} + q \cdot \tfrac{p}{q}\right) = \left(\tfrac{q}{p}\right)^{S_n} ,
$$

the bracket being $q+p=1$. Optional stopping then reads

$$
\left(\frac{q}{p}\right)^{i} = \left(\frac{q}{p}\right)^{N} P_i + 1 - P_i ,
$$

which rearranges to the same answer. All the work has moved into spotting the martingale, and $q/p$ is precisely the characteristic root of the difference equation above.

### An unbounded opponent

Letting $N \to \infty$,

$$
P_i \longrightarrow \begin{cases} 1 - (q/p)^{\,i}, & p > q, \\ 0, & p \le q .\end{cases}
$$

Against an opponent of unlimited means the gambler is ruined with certainty unless the game is favourable, a fair game included. At $p = \tfrac12$ the chance of ever reaching $N$ is only $i/N$, so the ruin is slow but sure.
